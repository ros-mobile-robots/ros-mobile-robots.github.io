    Localization: Wheel Odometry and IMU (EKF)

    DiffBot and Remo estimate how far they drove and turned from their wheels: the drive controller turns the wheel encoders' angles into odometry, a pose in the odom frame and a velocity in the robot's own frame, base_footprint. The pose is the position \(x\), \(y\) and the heading \(\theta\). The velocity is the forward speed \(v_x\) and the yaw rate \(\omega_z\), the angular velocity about the upward axis. REP 105's odom frame is ROS's convention for such a frame: the robot's pose in it changes smoothly, without jumps, but drifts over time. The wheels' estimate of turning is the weaker part. A wheel that slips, or a wheel separation that is a little off in the configuration, makes the estimated turn too small or too large, and the error in the heading \(\theta\) grows into an error in the position the further the robot drives.

    The IMU's gyroscope measures the turn directly: its yaw rate \(\omega_z\) doesn't depend on the wheels. With ekf:=true, an extended Kalman filter (EKF) from the robot_localization package combines both: it takes the forward speed \(v_x\) from the wheels and the yaw rate \(\omega_z\) mostly from the gyroscope, because the gyroscope's variance \(\sigma^2\) is far smaller. A variance says how far a value's errors spread. The smaller the variance, the more the filter trusts the value. The filter publishes its estimate on the topic /diffbot/odometry/filtered and as the transform odom → base_footprint on the topic /tf, which navigation and RViz use.

    Not tested on the robot yet

    The EKF runs in the simulation and in the tests below. On Remo, the IMU's rate, axes and noise passed the bench checks, but the EKF itself hasn't run on a robot yet. Remo's URDF model in remo_description includes the IMU's checked position and orientation (bench checks). DiffBot uses the same BNO055, but its mounting and the firmware still need checking on DiffBot. The variances that the firmware and the drive controller publish on the robot, and the IMU sample validator's time limits, are starting values, not measurements.

    The same as vectors, with the symbols of the filter's state. \(\mathbf{z}\) stands for a measurement and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) for the filter's estimate.

    The wheel odometry's measurement is the three velocities that the filter fuses from one odometry message:

    \[ \mathbf{z}_\text{wheels} = (\,v_x,\ v_y,\ \omega_z\,) \]

    The IMU's measurement is the yaw rate of one validated sample:

    \[ \mathbf{z}_\text{IMU} = (\,\omega_z\,) \]

    The estimate that the filter publishes is the robot's pose in the odom frame and its velocities in the frame base_footprint. These six values can differ from 0. two_d_mode keeps the other values of the published message at 0:

    \[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\text{published} = (\,x,\ y,\ \theta,\ v_x,\ v_y,\ \omega_z\,) \]
    • In, on DiffBot and Remo: two measurements. The wheel odometry gives the forward speed \(v_x\) and the yaw rate \(\omega_z\) that the drive controller computes from the wheel encoders, and the sideways speed \(v_y\), which the controller sets to 0 for a differential drive. The IMU gives the yaw rate \(\omega_z\) that its gyroscope measures. What the filter fuses says why the filter takes these values and no others.
    • In, possible but not fed here: robot_localization can fuse any number of inputs of four kinds, which are odometry, poses, velocities and IMUs (preparing the sensor data). Examples are a GPS position \(x\), \(y\), which its navsat_transform_node converts first, a pose \(x\), \(y\), \(\theta\) from a camera, a second IMU, and more odometry, for example visual odometry. On DiffBot and Remo, the filter gets none of these.
    • The estimate: robot_localization's state has 15 values: the position, the orientation, their velocities and the linear acceleration, each in three dimensions. two_d_mode keeps the height, roll, pitch, their velocities and the vertical acceleration at 0, for a robot on a flat floor. Of what remains, the filter publishes \(x\), \(y\), \(\theta\), \(v_x\), \(v_y\) and \(\omega_z\).
    • Out: the filter publishes its estimate in two forms. The topic /diffbot/odometry/filtered carries a nav_msgs/Odometry message: the pose \(x\), \(y\), \(\theta\) in the odom frame, the velocities \(v_x\), \(v_y\), \(\omega_z\) in the robot's frame base_footprint, and their covariances. The transform odom → base_footprint on the topic /tf carries the same pose.
    • Who uses it: with ekf:=true, the planners move_base and Move Base Flex read the robot's velocity from the odometry topic. RViz can show the topic with an Odometry display. The transform reaches everything that works with TF: RViz, amcl, which localizes the robot on a map, and the planners' costmaps.

    How it fits together

    The second figure shows the same flow as the ROS nodes and topics of DiffBot and Remo.

    graph TB subgraph Teensy [Teensy] IMU[BNO055] --> FW[firmware] end FW -->|imu/data_raw| VALIDATOR[imu_validator.py] VALIDATOR -->|imu/data_validated| EKF[ekf_localization] DDC[mobile_base_controller] -->|mobile_base_controller/odom| EKF EKF -->|odometry/filtered| NAV[move_base] EKF -->|TF| TF[odom to base_footprint]

    The topics are in the /diffbot namespace, for example /diffbot/imu/data_raw.

    • The firmware reads the BNO055 on the Teensy and publishes /diffbot/imu/data_raw at 50 Hz, as IMU: Bosch BNO055 explains. In the simulation, Gazebo's IMU sensor and a small adapter publish the same topic, as the Simulation page's IMU section explains.
    • The IMU sample validator, imu_validator.py, subscribes to /diffbot/imu/data_raw. It checks each sample against what this EKF configuration needs and forwards the valid samples, unchanged, on /diffbot/imu/data_validated. Why the EKF needs the IMU sample validator lists its checks.
    • The drive controller, mobile_base_controller (diff_drive_controller), publishes the wheel odometry. With the EKF on, it doesn't publish the transform odom → base_footprint: two nodes publishing the same transform would make the robot's pose jump between their two estimates.
    • The EKF, /diffbot/ekf_localization, subscribes to /diffbot/imu/data_validated and to the wheel odometry. It fuses the two and publishes the filtered odometry and the transform.

    ekf.launch in diffbot_control starts the validator and the EKF, for the robot and the simulation alike.

    Running it

    The ekf argument switches the EKF on. The EKF is off by default. Pass ekf:=true to the launch file you start:

    Where Command
    On the robot, when you start the bringup yourself roslaunch diffbot_bringup bringup.launch model:=remo ekf:=true (or bringup_with_laser.launch)
    In the simulation roslaunch diffbot_gazebo diffbot.launch model:=remo ekf:=true
    Simulation with navigation roslaunch diffbot_navigation diffbot.launch model:=remo ekf:=true
    Navigation on the development PC, for a robot that runs with the EKF roslaunch diffbot_navigation diffbot_hw.launch model:=remo ekf:=true

    Which launch file starts what

    A roslaunch command names one launch file. roslaunch reads that file and every file that it includes with the <include> tag, directly or through another included file. It starts the nodes that these files declare. A node is a running ROS program. A file can pass arguments such as ekf to a file that it includes. Starting an included file on its own doesn't read the file that includes it.

    The two figures show which launch file includes which, and which file declares which node. They don't show the order in which the nodes start. The pill-shaped box is your command. A rectangle is a launch file, written as its package and its file name. A rounded rectangle names the node or the two nodes that a file starts. A thick arrow is an include that passes ekf to the included file.

    On the robot, when you start the bringup yourself, you start one of the two bringup files. This command is for Remo. For DiffBot, use model:=diffbot:

    1
    roslaunch diffbot_bringup bringup.launch model:=remo ekf:=true

    With the robot container

    In the robot container, Docker Compose starts bringup.launch for you, without ekf, as the bringup service's command in compose.yaml says. So robot_state_publisher runs there and the EKF is off. The container's settings have no switch for the EKF. Don't start a second bringup by hand next to the container's.

    This figure leaves out the other nodes too. diffbot_gazebo/launch/diffbot.launch also includes diffbot_view.launch, which starts Gazebo through gazebo_ros's empty_world.launch. Gazebo runs the drive controller.

    What the file at the end of the thick arrows does with ekf: on the robot, that file is diffbot_base/launch/diffbot.launch. In the simulation, that file is diffbot_control.launch. Each of these two launch files loads the drive controller's configuration and does two things with its ekf value:

    1. The file sets the controller's enable_odom_tf parameter to false with the EKF and to true without it. The file writes the value in both cases because a value stays on the ROS parameter server as long as the ROS master runs. Without the true, a launch without the EKF after one with the EKF would keep the controller's transform off.
    2. The file includes ekf.launch, which starts the validator and the EKF, only if ekf is true.

    Why the EKF needs robot_state_publisher, the orange node: robot_state_publisher publishes the transforms of the joints in the robot's model, the URDF. From them, TF gets the transform between the IMU's frame imu_link and base_footprint. The EKF needs this transform to turn the IMU's yaw rate into the robot's frame.

    The navigation launch files pass ekf on in the same way. diffbot_navigation/launch/diffbot.launch includes diffbot_gazebo's diffbot.launch and move_base.launch and passes ekf to both. With ekf:=true, the planners in move_base.launch and in Move Base Flex's move_base.launch.xml read /diffbot/odometry/filtered instead of the controller's odometry, so that their velocities and the transform come from the same estimate. diffbot_hw.launch starts no EKF. Its ekf argument only selects the odometry that the planner reads, for a robot whose bringup runs with ekf:=true.

    To see what the filter does, compare the two odometries while the robot turns, for example in RViz with two Odometry displays, or with rostopic echo /diffbot/odometry/filtered/twist/twist/angular next to /diffbot/mobile_base_controller/odom's.

    What the filter fuses

    ekf.yaml configures the filter. For each input, a list of 15 booleans says which values of the input's messages the filter fuses: odom0_config for the wheel odometry and imu0_config for the IMU. To fuse a value means that the filter corrects its estimate of one variable with the measured value. How far the estimate moves towards the measurement depends on two uncertainties: the measurement's variance \(\sigma^2\) and the uncertainty of the filter's own estimate. The smaller the measurement's variance is next to the estimate's uncertainty, the closer the estimate moves to the measurement.

    The filter's state

    The 15 booleans stand for the 15 variables that robot_localization's filter estimates, its state \(\mathbf{x}\):

    \[ \mathbf{x} = (\,x,\ y,\ z,\ \text{roll},\ \text{pitch},\ \theta,\ v_x,\ v_y,\ v_z,\ \omega_x,\ \omega_y,\ \omega_z,\ a_x,\ a_y,\ a_z\,) \]

    The table shows the state's five groups of three variables, with the labels that the comments in ekf.yaml use and the names on robot_localization's parameter page, which gives the same order:

    Group Symbols Labels in ekf.yaml Names on the parameter page
    Position \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) x, y, z \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\)
    Orientation roll, pitch, heading \(\theta\) roll, pitch, yaw roll, pitch, yaw
    Linear velocity \(v_x\), \(v_y\), \(v_z\) vx, vy, vz \(\dot X\), \(\dot Y\), \(\dot Z\)
    Angular velocity \(\omega_x\), \(\omega_y\), \(\omega_z\) vroll, vpitch, vyaw the rates of roll, pitch and yaw
    Linear acceleration \(a_x\), \(a_y\), \(a_z\) ax, ay, az \(\ddot X\), \(\ddot Y\), \(\ddot Z\)

    The position and the orientation are the robot's pose in the filter's world_frame, here odom. The velocities and the acceleration are in the robot's own frame, the filter's base_link_frame, here base_footprint. The yaw rate \(\omega_z\) is the rate at which the heading \(\theta\) changes.

      • Message field: angular_velocity.z
      • Variance: angular_velocity_covariance[8], 4 · 10⁻⁴ (rad/s)² on the robot and 2.5 · 10⁻⁵ (rad/s)² in the simulation
      • Frame: imu_link, rotated into base_footprint

    What the list's three entries mean:

    • The messages: the wheel odometry arrives as nav_msgs/Odometry messages on the topic mobile_base_controller/odom. The IMU's samples arrive as sensor_msgs/Imu messages on the topic imu/data_validated.
    • The variances: a message stores a covariance matrix row by row. twist.covariance has 6 × 6 elements for the three linear and the three angular velocities, so the variances on its diagonal are the elements 0, 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35. angular_velocity_covariance has 3 × 3 elements, with the variances at 0, 4 and 8. The wheel odometry's variances are the drive controller's twist_covariance_diagonal in diffbot_control.yaml, on the robot and in the simulation. The IMU's variance on the robot is a fixed value in the firmware's configuration. In the simulation, the IMU's variance is the square of gaussianNoise, 0.005 rad/s, the noise that the simulated sensor adds to each value.
    • The frames: the filter turns every velocity into its base_link_frame, base_footprint, before it fuses the value. The wheel odometry names the frame of its velocities in child_frame_id, which is base_footprint already. An IMU sample names its frame in header.frame_id, imu_link, and the filter rotates the angular velocity into base_footprint with the transform from TF. In the URDF models of both robots, the z axis of imu_link points up like the robot's, so the rotation leaves angular_velocity.z as it is.

    Why these values and no others

    Input Fused Not fused, and why
    Wheel odometry forward speed \(v_x\), sideways speed \(v_y\), yaw rate \(\omega_z\) its pose \(x\), \(y\), \(\theta\): the controller computes the pose from the same velocities, so fusing both would count the wheels twice
    IMU yaw rate \(\omega_z\) its orientation, which the firmware marks as unavailable in the message, and its acceleration, which would need its bias removed first

    The sideways speed \(v_y\) is always 0 in the wheel odometry, because a differential drive robot can't move sideways. Fusing the 0 keeps the estimate from drifting sideways. Both inputs give the yaw rate \(\omega_z\). On the robot, the gyroscope's variance of 4 · 10⁻⁴ (rad/s)² is 75 times smaller than the wheels' 0.03 (rad/s)², so the filter's yaw rate lies close to the gyroscope's. In the simulation, the simulated gyroscope's variance is 1200 times smaller than the wheels'.

For every measurement, the filter replaces the measurement's value for each of the seven with 0, gives the 0 the small variance 10⁻⁶, and marks the variable as fused.     The other settings

    • odom0_differential and imu0_differential: the _differential setting applies to pose values only. With true, the filter would fuse the change between two consecutive poses as a velocity, instead of the pose. Neither list fuses a pose value, so the setting has no effect here. false is robot_localization's default.
    • odom0_queue_size and imu0_queue_size: the queue size of the filter's ROS subscriber for the input's topic. Up to 10 messages wait in the queue until the filter node takes them. robot_localization's default is 1.
    • world_frame: is odom, so the estimate is continuous, without the jumps of a map-based localization such as amcl, which publishes map → odom itself.
    • frequency: is 50 Hz, the IMU's rate. In the simulation, the wheel odometry arrives at 50 Hz too, the drive controller's publish_rate in diffbot_control.yaml. On the robot, the wheel odometry arrives at most 10 times per second: the base node's control loop in diffbot_base.cpp runs the controller at 10 Hz, and the controller publishes at most one odometry message each time it runs.

    The IMU sample validator

    The IMU sample validator is the ROS node /diffbot/imu_validator, from imu_validator.py. It subscribes to the topic /diffbot/imu/data_raw and forwards each valid sample, unchanged and with its stamp, on the topic /diffbot/imu/data_validated, which the EKF reads. It drops every other sample.

    Why the EKF needs the validator

    robot_localization's own stamp check for an input allows equal stamps and doesn't limit a sample's age. In robot_localization 2.7.7, the version in the dev container, the IMU's angular velocity passes this check when its stamp msg->header.stamp isn't earlier than lastMessageTimes_, the last stamp on the same input. When a measurement's stamp isn't later than the last measurement that the filter processed, delta is not positive, and the filter skips predict() and corrects its current estimate with the measurement. ekf.yaml leaves smooth_lagged_data off, so the filter doesn't go back to the time of a late measurement.

    So a delayed sample, or one with the same stamp as the one before, can pass the filter's stamp check. If the sample also passes the filter's other input checks, such as the transform into base_footprint, the filter corrects its current estimate with the sample, as if the sample were current. A sample that waited in a buffer would count as the robot's yaw rate \(\omega_z\) now.

    The five checks

    A sample is valid only if it passes all five checks of the list below the figure. For each sample, the node calls validate_and_record() of the class ImuValidator, which runs the checks in this order and returns the reason of the first one that fails. In the figure and in the list, \(t\) is the ROS time, the node's clock at the moment the sample arrives. \(t_s\) is the sample's stamp, and \(t_\text{last}\) is the stamp of the last sample that the node forwarded.

    1. The frame, check_frame(): the sample's header.frame_id is the node's parameter frame_id, imu_link. The EKF needs this frame to turn the measured rate into the robot's frame with TF.
    2. The values, check_finite_values(): the three components of the angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and of the linear acceleration \(\mathbf f\) are finite numbers, so none is NaN or infinite.
    3. The variances, check_angular_velocity_covariance(): the nine values of the angular velocity's covariance are finite, and its diagonal, the three variances \(\sigma^2\), is positive.
    4. The stamp against the ROS time, check_not_too_old() and check_not_from_the_future(): \(t - \texttt{max\_age} \le t_s \le t + \texttt{max\_future}\). A stamp can fail only one of the two, so their order doesn't matter.
      • Not too old: the stamp is at most max_age, 0.1 s, older than the ROS time. That is five samples at the IMU's 50 Hz. On the robot, a sample takes time to travel from the Teensy over USB and through rosserial's node. So its stamp is normally a little older than the ROS time when the sample arrives, as sample B in the figure. A sample that waited long in a buffer on the way is older than max_age, as sample A.
      • Not too far ahead: the stamp is at most max_future, 0.02 s, ahead of the ROS time. That is one sample. The Teensy stamps a sample with its own clock, which rosserial synchronizes only approximately with the SBC's clock. The offset that remains between the two clocks can put a stamp ahead of the ROS time, as sample C. In the simulation, a sample can arrive before the node has received the matching message on the topic /clock. Sample D is ahead by more than max_future. max_future is the allowed difference, not a measured delay.
    5. The stamp against the last forwarded sample, check_newer_than_the_last(): \(t_s > t_\text{last}\). A sample with an older stamp arrived out of order, as sample E. A sample with the same stamp came twice, as sample F.

    Before the first check, the node drops every sample while the ROS time is zero. With simulation time, the ROS time is zero until the first message on Gazebo's topic /clock arrives. When the ROS time goes back, as when Gazebo's world is reset, the order check starts over from the new time.

    frame_id, max_age and max_future are the node's parameters, which ekf.launch sets. The values of max_age and max_future are initial policy values, not standard IMU limits. They stay until the latency of the robot's USB link and rosserial is measured.

    What the validator doesn't check

    • Other fields of the message: the orientation, the acceleration's covariance, or whether a covariance matrix is positive definite. So the validator doesn't validate every field of a sensor_msgs/Imu message.
    • Whether a value is plausible: the validator looks at a sample's form and its time only. A yaw rate \(\omega_z\) of 100 rad/s with a fresh stamp is valid.

    The validator also never gives an old sample a new stamp.

    Judging a value is a statistical test inside robot_localization, which a rejection threshold switches on. Each input can have a threshold for its pose update, for its velocity update and for its acceleration update. With a threshold, the filter doesn't fuse an update that lies too far from the filter's own prediction. The filter measures "too far" as a Mahalanobis distance: the difference between the measurement and the prediction, counted in units of their combined uncertainty. For an update of one value, the distance is the number of standard deviations between the measurement and the prediction. For an update of several values, the distance is one number for all of them together, not a limit for each value. The test compares an input with the filter's prediction, not with the other input.

    ekf.yaml sets no rejection threshold, so this test is off for both inputs. The filter's other handling of an input stays: the stamp-order check, the transform into base_footprint, the selection by the two lists, and leaving out values that are not finite. The test is off for two reasons:

    • The robot's variances are starting values: the firmware and the drive controller publish fixed variances. Whether these fit while the robot drives isn't checked yet. A threshold counts in these uncertainties, so a number chosen today would mean little.
    • A threshold needs recorded drives: a threshold that is too tight rejects valid changes of the motion, for example the start of a turn. Recordings at rest can't show how the test behaves when the robot accelerates, turns and slips.

    diffbot#205 evaluates the thresholds on recorded drives of the robot.

    How robot_localization 2.7.7 computes the test, and the parameters' names

    Once the filter has its first measurement, which sets the state without a test, the filter compares the squared distance \(d^2\) of every update with the square of the threshold:

    \[ d^2 = \mathbf{y}^\mathsf{T} \left(\mathbf{H} \mathbf{P} \mathbf{H}^\mathsf{T} + \mathbf{R}\right)^{-1} \mathbf{y}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H} \hat{\mathbf{x}} \]
    • \(\mathbf{z}\): the values of this update after the transform into base_footprint, for example the three velocities of one wheel odometry message, together with the zeros that two_d_mode adds.
    • \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\): the filter's state right before this correction. The filter predicts the state to the measurement's stamp when that stamp is later than the filter's time. A late measurement is compared with the state as it is.
    • \(\mathbf{H}\): the matrix that picks the update's variables out of the state.
    • \(\mathbf{y}\): the innovation, the difference between the measurement and the state.
    • \(\mathbf{P}\): the covariance of that state, the filter's own uncertainty.
    • \(\mathbf{R}\): the covariance of the update's values after the transform, together with the variances that two_d_mode adds.
    • The raised \(\mathsf{T}\) transposes a vector or a matrix, and the raised \(-1\) inverts a matrix.

    The filter doesn't fuse the update when \(d^2\) is the threshold's square or more. The function checkMahalanobisThreshold() computes sqMahalanobis, \(d^2\), from innovation, \(\mathbf{y}\), and invCovariance, the inverse in the formula. nsigmas is the threshold.     The validator's diagnostics

    The node publishes its counts on the topic /diagnostics, next to the EKF's own status there: how many samples it forwarded, and how many it dropped for which reason. The function health() sets the status. The status is an error when no sample was forwarded for more than SILENCE_LIMIT, 1 s, or since the ROS time last went back. It is a warning when the node dropped samples since its last report. rosrun rqt_robot_monitor rqt_robot_monitor shows the status and the counts.

    Limits

    • A clock that goes back: in the simulation, the filter starts over (reset_on_time_jump). robot_localization does that only with simulated time. On the robot, after the computer's clock goes back, the filter ignores each input until its stamps are newer than its last ones again.
    • A reset of the simulation: /gazebo/reset_simulation sets the simulation's clock back to zero. The simulated IMU, the validator and the filter go on from the new time right away. The drive controller's odometry doesn't. diff_drive_controller publishes again only when the clock has passed last_state_publish_time_, the time of its last message from before the reset. The excerpt of diff_drive_controller.cpp shows the condition. Until then, the filter gets the IMU's yaw rate but no wheel speeds. In a test run that was reset after 9.7 s, the filter's first output after the reset was stamped 0.08 s, and the wheel odometry's first message 9.75 s.
    • Starting values: the variances that the firmware and the drive controller publish on the robot, and the IMU sample validator's time limits, are not measured yet. The bench checks measured the IMU's noise at rest. Measurements while driving still have to show the values that the EKF should use. In the simulation, the IMU's variance describes the Gaussian noise that the simulated sensor adds.
    • Drift: the pose drifts over time, as any odometry. The gyroscope helps with the heading, but nothing corrects the position. A map-based localization such as amcl corrects it in the map frame.
