    Wheel Dynamics in Simulation

    A simulated robot can show effects that its kinematic model can't explain:

    • Its wheels turn exactly as commanded, but the robot turns less than its odometry says.
    • Its wheels jitter when it turns on the spot.
    • A higher motor gain, meant to follow the commands better, makes the model explode at spawn.

    All three come from parts of the simulation that the kinematic model and odometry leave out: where the wheels touch the ground, how the physics engine computes the contact forces, and how a simulated motor turns a velocity command into a torque. This page explains these parts and the parameters that set them. It uses the setup of DiffBot and Remo, ROS 1 Noetic with Gazebo Classic 11 and its default physics engine, ODE (Open Dynamics Engine). The new Gazebo, ROS 2 and other physics engines name and implement some of these parameters differently.

    DiffBot's and Remo's current values, their source files and a tuning procedure are on the Simulation page, in Tuning the simulated robot. The Simulation Test shows how these parameters affect the measured motion.

    A wheel in the robot description

    A URDF link has three separate descriptions (URDF link specification):

    • Visual: what you see in RViz and Gazebo, often a mesh. The physics engine doesn't use it.
    • Collision: the geometry that the physics engine checks for contacts. Its shape decides where the wheel touches the ground.
    • Inertial: the mass, the center of mass and the inertia tensor. They decide how the wheel accelerates under a torque.

    They don't have to agree. DiffBot's and Remo's wheels look like their mesh, collide as a simple shape and have the inertia of a solid cylinder.

    The collision shape decides where the contacts are

    On flat ground, a cylinder touches along a line across its width, and the physics engine reduces that line to a few contact points. A sphere with the wheel's radius touches at one point in the wheel's center plane.

    Where the contact forces act matters for turning. The kinematic model assumes that each wheel pushes on the ground in its center plane, so the turn rate is \(\omega = (v_r - v_l) / L\), with the wheel separation \(L\) between the wheel centers. If the forces act at the wheels' outer edges instead, the robot turns as if \(L\) were one wheel width larger.

    Both robots showed exactly this with cylinder collisions. DiffBot turned on the spot only about 85 % of its commanded angle, the ratio of its wheel separation to the distance between its wheels' outer edges, 0.138 m / 0.163 m = 0.85 (diffbot#120). Remo turned 1.28 of 1.50 rad, 0.144 m / 0.169 m = 0.85 (remo_description#2). Remo's cylinders only seemed to turn it correctly while its wheels were allowed to sink into the ground (below). With sphere collisions, both turn as their odometry says, DiffBot within 0.03 rad and Remo within 0.002 rad. The sphere is still a modeling choice, which the simulation test checks.

    A sphere also has limits: it only represents an upright wheel on flat ground. On a slope or an edge it can touch the ground beside the real tire. And the collision shape doesn't change the inertia: a wheel with a sphere collision still has the inertia of its real shape, here a cylinder.

    Mass and inertia

    For a wheel that spins about its axle, the inertia that matters is the one about that axis. A solid cylinder of mass \(m\) and radius \(r\) has

    \[ J = \frac{1}{2} m r^2 . \]

    A torque \(\tau\) on the wheel accelerates it at \(\dot{\omega} = \tau / J\), so a light wheel responds faster to the same torque. That is good for following commands, and it also makes the velocity loop easier to destabilize (below). Remo's 32 g wheels with a radius of 0.033 m have \(J = 0.5 \cdot 0.032 \cdot 0.033^2 = 1.74 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\).

    The inertia tensor is given in the inertial frame. A URDF cylinder's axis is its z axis, while the wheel's joint axis is its y axis, so the inertial origin has to rotate the cylinder's \(\frac{1}{2} m r^2\) onto the joint axis. A wrong rotation gives the wheel the smaller inertia about a diameter, \(\frac{1}{12} m (3r^2 + l^2)\), which doesn't show in RViz but changes its dynamics. Modern Robotics, section 8.2, explains the rotational inertia of a rigid body and how it changes with the frame.

    Contact and friction

    ODE treats a contact as a constraint: the two bodies must not move into each other, and the friction force must not exceed its limit. Gazebo creates these constraints from the collision shapes in each time step, with the parameters below. The ODE user guide and Gazebo's Friction and Physics Parameters tutorials describe them in full.

    Friction

    Coulomb's friction model limits the tangential contact force \(f_t\) by the normal force \(f_n\):

    \[ \lVert f_t \rVert \le \mu \, f_n . \]

    The allowed forces form a cone. ODE approximates the cone by a pyramid with two directions in the contact plane, with one coefficient each (ODE user guide, section 3.11.1):

    \[ \lvert f_1 \rvert \le \mu_1 f_n , \qquad \lvert f_2 \rvert \le \mu_2 f_n . \]

    mu1 and mu2 are these two coefficients, not a static and a dynamic friction coefficient. fdir1 sets the first direction, in the collision's own frame; without it, ODE picks the two directions itself. With mu1 = mu2, as on both robots, the pyramid is symmetric and fdir1 isn't needed.

    fdir1 is easy to get wrong on a wheel. Gazebo turns it with the collision's pose (ODEPhysics.cc), and ODE uses it as given, without projecting it onto the contact plane (contact.cpp). In a URDF, a wheel's collision cylinder is usually rotated so that its z axis is the axle, which doesn't move as the wheel spins, so fdir1 0 0 1 stays along the axle. A sphere has no such rotation: its frame spins with the wheel, and the same fdir1 tilts into the contact normal. With a sphere and fdir1 0 0 1, Remo drove about 2 % less than its odometry when driving straight; without fdir1, it drives as far as its odometry says.

    For each contact, Gazebo uses the smaller coefficient of the two surfaces (ODEPhysics.cc). Gazebo's ground plane has mu 100 and mu2 50, so the wheel's coefficients decide.

    Friction limits the force; it doesn't make a wheel roll. Higher friction doesn't fix a wrong wheel speed or a wrong geometry. Remo's wheels jittered when it turned on the spot, with cylinder collisions and friction 200. The cause was where the cylinders touched the ground: with spheres, they turn smoothly even with friction 200. Remo now uses friction 1, closer to rubber on a floor.

    Contact stiffness and damping

    In each time step, ODE corrects a part of the penetration between two bodies. Two parameters control how hard a constraint is (ODE user guide, sections 3.7 and 3.8):

    • ERP, the error reduction parameter, between 0 and 1: the fraction of the penetration that is corrected in the next step.
    • CFM, the constraint force mixing: 0 makes the constraint hard; a positive value lets the bodies press into each other, more the larger it is.

    A spring with stiffness \(k_p\) and a damper with damping \(k_d\) correspond to (ODE user guide, section 3.8.2)

    \[ \mathrm{ERP} = \frac{h k_p}{h k_p + k_d}, \qquad \mathrm{CFM} = \frac{1}{h k_p + k_d}, \]

    where \(h\) is the physics time step. ODE then behaves like that spring and damper, integrated with an implicit first-order method. The kp and kd of a Gazebo surface are these spring and damping coefficients. They describe the contact, not the motor, and have nothing to do with the P and D gains of the wheel's velocity controller.

    For each contact, Gazebo combines the two surfaces like a spring and a damper in series: \(k_p = 1 / (1/k_{p,1} + 1/k_{p,2})\) and \(k_d = k_{d,1} + k_{d,2}\). It computes ERP and CFM with the world's time step and uses them for this contact instead of the world's <erp> and <cfm> (ODEPhysics.cc). For a wheel on Gazebo's ground plane, with \(h\) = 1 ms:

    Wheel kp, kd Ground kp, kd Combined \(k_p\), \(k_d\) ERP CFM
    DiffBot Gazebo's defaults, \(10^{12}\), 1 \(10^{12}\), 1 \(5 \cdot 10^{11}\), 2 1.0 \(2 \cdot 10^{-9}\)
    Remo \(10^{7}\), 1 \(10^{12}\), 1 \(10^{7}\), 2 0.9998 \(10^{-4}\)

    Remo's contacts are softer and damped. On a sphere's single contact point and with minDepth 0 (see below), its earlier stiff and undamped contacts (\(10^{12}\), 0) with friction 200 let the wheels miss their speed commands by up to 30 %; with the damped contacts, they follow them.

    These are equivalences, not material properties: kp and kd set ERP and CFM, and it doesn't help to treat the two pairs as independent settings. A real tire's stiffness is a starting point at most.

    Two more parameters limit the correction:

    • minDepth: the penetration that isn't corrected, in meters.
    • maxVel: the maximum velocity at which a penetration is corrected, in m/s. It isn't a speed limit for the wheel or the robot.

    For a wheel on the static ground plane, ODE uses the wheel's own minDepth and maxVel, and limits maxVel by the world's contact_max_correcting_vel (contact.cpp, lines 222–237).

    minDepth and soft contacts don't go together. Up to minDepth, ODE doesn't correct the penetration at all, and a positive CFM lets the wheel press further into the ground under its load. With Remo's soft contacts, its wheels sank slowly until they reached minDepth: their centers were 8.3 mm lower than the wheel radius after a minute at rest with minDepth 0.01 m, a quarter of the radius, and 1.0 mm lower with 0.001 m. With 0, as now, they stay at the wheel radius. DiffBot's stiff default contacts don't sink. The sinking had also hidden the cylinders' outer-edge contacts: with minDepth 0 and cylinders, Remo turned 15 % too little.

    From URDF to SDF

    Gazebo converts the URDF to SDF, its own format. The <gazebo reference="..."> elements of a link set its collision's surface (Using a URDF in Gazebo). gz sdf -p robot.urdf shows the result:

    URDF <gazebo> element SDF element in collision/surface Meaning Default
    mu1, mu2 friction/ode/mu, mu2 friction coefficients in the two pyramid directions 1, 1
    fdir1 friction/ode/fdir1 first friction direction, in the collision frame none (0 0 0)
    kp, kd contact/ode/kp, kd contact stiffness (N/m) and damping (N s/m) \(10^{12}\), 1
    minDepth contact/ode/min_depth penetration that isn't corrected (m) 0
    maxVel contact/ode/max_vel maximum correction velocity (m/s) 0.01

    The defaults are from the SDFormat 1.7 specification that Gazebo 11 uses (surface.sdf).

    The simulated motor: from velocity command to torque

    On the real robot, diff_drive_controller computes the two wheel speeds from /cmd_vel, and the motor PID in the firmware makes the motors reach them. In Gazebo, gazebo_ros_control's DefaultRobotHWSim takes the place of the firmware:

    graph LR C[diff_drive_controller] -- "wheel speed command ω*" --> PID[DefaultRobotHWSim: velocity PID]; PID -- torque τ --> W[Wheel: inertia J]; G[Ground contact: friction, kp, kd] -- contact forces --> W; W -- "measured wheel speed ω" --> PID; W -- joint states --> C;

    If the parameter server has gains for a wheel joint under /<robot namespace>/gazebo_ros_control/pid_gains/<joint>, DefaultRobotHWSim controls the joint with a PID that computes a torque from the speed error and applies it to the joint (default_robot_hw_sim.cpp, lines 370–380). Without gains, it sets the joint's velocity directly, a different behavior. DiffBot and Remo load gains.

    The PID is control_toolbox's Pid (pid.cpp). With the speed error \(e = \omega^* - \omega\) in rad/s, it computes the torque

    \[ \tau = P e + I \int e \, dt + D \dot{e} , \]

    in N m, so P is in N m s/rad and I in N m/rad. Its parameters:

    • p, i, d: the gains. A P gain alone needs a speed error to produce a torque, so a wheel that needs torque, for example to turn the robot on the spot against friction, stays slower than commanded. The I term grows until it supplies that torque, and the error goes to zero.
    • i_clamp: limits the I term's torque to ±i_clamp (N m), not the total torque. It defaults to 0, so an I gain without i_clamp does nothing.
    • antiwindup: with true, the integral of the error stops growing once the I term reaches i_clamp. With false, the I term is still limited, but the integral keeps growing and takes longer to come back when the error changes sign.

    The PID Controllers page and Feedback Systems, chapter 11, explain PID control, integral windup and tuning in general.

    DefaultRobotHWSim limits the torque to the joint's effort limit, from the URDF's <limit effort="..."> or the joint_limits parameters (lines 404–454). DiffBot's and Remo's wheel joints have neither, so their simulated motors have no torque limit, unlike a real motor.

    Time step and stability of the velocity loop

    Gazebo updates the wheel torque once per physics step \(h\). Take a wheel that doesn't touch the ground, as at spawn or when it slips, and only a P gain. Within one step, the torque is constant:

    \[ e_k = \omega^* - \omega_k , \qquad \tau_k = P e_k , \qquad \omega_{k+1} = \omega_k + \frac{h}{J} \tau_k . \]

    With a constant command \(\omega^*\), the error in the next step is

    \[ e_{k+1} = \left( 1 - \frac{P h}{J} \right) e_k . \]

    The error decays only if this factor's magnitude is below 1:

    \[ \left| 1 - \frac{P h}{J} \right| < 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 0 < \frac{P h}{J} < 2 . \]

    At \(P h / J = 2\), the error changes sign in every step without decaying; above it, it grows in every step. A continuous-time P loop on an inertia has no such upper limit. The limit comes from holding the torque over a step, which is typical of sampled systems; Franklin, Powell and Workman's Digital Control of Dynamic Systems treats them in depth.

    For Remo, \(J = 1.74 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(h\) = 1 ms give \(P < 2J/h = 0.035\) N m s/rad. With P 0.03, the factor is \(1 - 0.03 \cdot 0.001 / 1.74 \cdot 10^{-5} = -0.72\): the error changes sign and decays. With P 0.06, it is \(-2.44\): the error grows, and Remo's model spawned at the origin with NaN velocities (diffbot#121). That is why Remo's P gain is 0.03. DiffBot's wheels are modeled with 0.5 kg (\(J = 2.8 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\)), so their limit is about 0.55, well above its P 0.06.

    The estimate assumes a lot: one free wheel with a constant inertia, only a P gain, fresh speed feedback in every step, and no torque limit, friction in the bearings, delay or contact forces. The actual loop has an I term, and a wheel on the ground also moves the robot. So a gain below the limit doesn't prove that the simulation is stable, and a gain above it doesn't mean that Gazebo must explode. It tells the scale at which the gain becomes a problem, and that the gain has to change when the wheel's mass or the time step changes.

    The time step is the physics step of the world, max_step_size (1 ms in DiffBot's worlds), not the rate of /cmd_vel or of the joint states. gazebo_ros_control writes the torque in every physics step, but reads the joint states and updates the controllers only every controlPeriod, which defaults to the physics step (gazebo_ros_control_plugin.cpp). A longer controlPeriod adds a delay that this estimate doesn't include.

    Further reading

    To go further on one topic, start with the official reference next to its explanation above, then one chapter of a book:

    • ODE user guide (ode.org) and Gazebo Classic's tutorials Friction, Physics Parameters, Using a URDF in Gazebo and ROS Control: the reference for the contact model and the parameters that Gazebo Classic actually uses.
    • Karl Johan Åström and Richard M. Murray, Feedback Systems: An Introduction for Scientists and Engineers, 2nd edition, Princeton University Press 2021 (free online at fbsbook.org; Amazon.com, Amazon.de, affiliate links): chapter 11, PID control, with integral windup and tuning.
    • Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, Modern Robotics: Mechanics, Planning, and Control, Cambridge University Press 2017 (free preprint and videos at modernrobotics.org; Amazon.com, Amazon.de, affiliate links): section 8.2 for the inertia of a rigid body, chapter 13 for wheeled mobile robots and their rolling constraints.

    For more depth:

    • Gene F. Franklin, J. David Powell and Michael Workman, Digital Control of Dynamic Systems, 3rd edition (e-edition at SC Solutions): sampled systems and the choice of the sample rate (chapters 3 to 5 and 11), the background of the stability estimate above.
    • David Baraff, Rigid Body Simulation II: Nonpenetration Constraints, SIGGRAPH course notes: how a simulator computes contact and friction forces as constraints. A concept, not ODE's exact solver.
    • Roland Siegwart, Illah R. Nourbakhsh and Davide Scaramuzza, Introduction to Autonomous Mobile Robots, 2nd edition, MIT Press 2011: wheel types and their rolling and sliding constraints, an alternative to Modern Robotics' chapter 13.
    • Roy Featherstone, Rigid Body Dynamics Algorithms, Springer 2008: the algorithms of physics engines, with a chapter on contact and impact. Advanced.
