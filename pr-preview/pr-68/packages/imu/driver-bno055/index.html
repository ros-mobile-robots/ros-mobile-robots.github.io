    IMU: Bosch BNO055

    An inertial measurement unit (IMU) measures how the robot turns and accelerates: its gyroscope measures the angular velocity about three axes, its accelerometer the specific force, the acceleration minus gravity, which at rest reads about +9.8 m/s² upwards. DiffBot and Remo use the Adafruit 9-DOF Absolute Orientation IMU Fusion Breakout with Bosch's BNO055: an accelerometer, a gyroscope and a magnetometer in one package, with a processor that fuses them.

    The robot uses one value from it: the yaw rate, the angular velocity about the upward axis. The EKF on the robot's computer fuses it with the wheel odometry, which estimates turns less well. The BNO055 sits on the Teensy's I2C bus, and the Teensy's firmware publishes its measurements over rosserial.

    Not tested on the robot yet

    The firmware, its unit tests and the simulated IMU run without hardware. The wiring below and the bench checks on the robot are still open, and so is where the board sits on the robot, which its URDF's imu_link has to match.

    Wiring to the Teensy

    The BNO055 shares the Teensy's first I2C bus, Wire, with the motor driver (Adafruit Motor FeatherWing). On the Teensy 4.0 and 3.2, Wire uses pin 18 for SDA and pin 19 for SCL (PJRC: Wire library):

    BNO055 breakout Teensy Note
    VIN 3.3V The breakout takes 3.3 to 5 V. 3.3 V is the Teensy's logic level
    GND GND
    SDA 18 (SDA) The breakout has a 10 kΩ pull-up resistor on SDA and SCL
    SCL 19 (SCL)
    ADR not connected I2C address 0x28. Connected to 3.3 V: 0x29, then set IMU_BNO055_ADDRESS

    The other pins (RST, INT, PS0, PS1) stay unconnected. Adafruit's pinouts page describes all of them. The motor driver's address is 0x60, so the two don't collide.

    What the firmware publishes

    The firmware in diffbot_firmware reads the BNO055 in its IMU fusion mode (IMUPLUS: accelerometer and gyroscope, without the magnetometer) and publishes /diffbot/imu/data_raw, a sensor_msgs/Imu message, at 50 Hz:

    Field Content
    header.frame_id imu_link: the sensor's own axes. The robot's URDF places this frame where the board sits
    header.stamp ROS time before the read
    angular_velocity rad/s, from the gyroscope
    linear_acceleration m/s², the specific force, including gravity
    orientation not provided: orientation_covariance[0] is −1, which REP 145 defines as "no data" for a field
    covariances diagonal starting values, not measured yet: 4 · 10⁻⁴ (rad/s)² and 10⁻² (m/s²)²

    The chip's own orientation estimate isn't read: the EKF uses only the angular velocity. The topic's name is REP 145's for accelerometer and gyroscope data without an orientation.

    The settings are in diffbot_base_config.h: IMU_ENABLED (0 for a robot without an IMU), IMU_BNO055_ADDRESS, the rate, the frame, the covariances, and the retry and time limits below. The comment there explains the covariances' starting values with the datasheet's noise and offsets.

    How the firmware reads it

    lib/imu has a small driver of its own for the BNO055, instead of Adafruit's library, so that every I2C transfer is checked and the start-up's waits don't hold up the firmware's loop:

    • The start-up doesn't block the motor control. A reset of the chip takes about 650 ms, and each switch of its operating mode up to 19 ms (Bosch's datasheet). The driver goes through the steps as a state machine: configuration mode, reset, settings written and read back, IMU mode, until the chip reports that its fusion runs. Instead of sleeping through those times, each pass through the firmware's loop checks whether the next step is due and does at most a few transfers. The transfers themselves are synchronous: the loop waits while one runs, about 0.3 ms for one register and about 3 ms for a whole sample (26 bytes) at I2C's standard clock of 100 kHz. Whether the control loop keeps its rate when the bus has a fault is one of the bench checks.
    • A sample is published only if it's good: the read succeeded and took at most IMU_READ_BUDGET_US (10 ms: a later result is dropped, but the budget can't cut a transfer short), the chip still is in IMU mode with the right units and its fusion runs. A stalled or unplugged IMU stops the topic instead of repeating old values with new stamps. After ten failed reads in a row, the firmware starts the chip again. A missing IMU is looked for every IMU_RETRY_PERIOD seconds (10 s). The robot drives without an IMU, too.
    • Stamps are ROS time, which rosserial takes from the robot's computer: the Teensy's clock is synchronized with it every few seconds. Until the first synchronization after a connection, the firmware publishes nothing, because a stamp would carry the Teensy's uptime instead of ROS time.
    • The 50 Hz rate counts on the Teensy's own clock (millis()), so it doesn't change when ROS time jumps, for example when the Teensy connects to a computer whose clock is earlier.

    Unit tests on the PC: pio test -e native in diffbot_firmware runs the driver, the bus code, the time-sync check and the publisher with a fake sensor, I2C bus, serial port and clock, and with rosserial's own message parser. CI runs them in the firmware workflow (Testing and CI). They check the logic, not the hardware.

    Bench checks

    With the bringup running on the robot and its wheels off the ground:

    1. Rate and stamps: rostopic hz /diffbot/imu/data_raw shows about 50 Hz, and the stamps in rostopic echo /diffbot/imu/data_raw are the current ROS time.
    2. Axes and signs: at rest, the acceleration's upward axis reads about +9.8 m/s². Turning the robot counterclockwise by hand, seen from above, gives a positive angular velocity about the upward axis (REP 103). Both decide how imu_link sits in the URDF.
    3. Noise and bias: a stationary recording of a few minutes (rosbag record /diffbot/imu/data_raw) gives the gyroscope's bias and variance at 50 Hz, to replace the covariances' starting values.
    4. Faults: unplugging the IMU stops the topic within a sample and logs a warning, and the wheels keep following commands. Plugged back in, it's found at the next try, at most IMU_RETRY_PERIOD (10 s) later, and publishes again after its start-up, which takes about another second. The control loop keeps its rate during all this. The command timeout keeps working: after 5 s without a wheel command, it sets the target speeds to zero, which doesn't guarantee yet that the motors stop (diffbot#146).
    5. Timing: the control loop keeps its rate with the IMU running, for example rostopic hz /diffbot/measured_joint_states.
    Trying the breakout on the Raspberry Pi

    To check a breakout on its own, it can also be connected to the Raspberry Pi's I2C pins (pinout): VIN to 3.3 V, GND to GND, SDA to SDA, SCL to SCL. Adafruit's guide reads it with Python and its CircuitPython library: first prepare the Pi for it (I2C on, the Blinka library), then slow down the Pi's I2C clock, which the BNO055 needs because it stretches the clock, and then run the BNO055 example.
