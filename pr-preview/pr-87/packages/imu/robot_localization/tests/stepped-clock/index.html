    Test: The EKF on a Stepped Clock

    test_ekf_synthetic.py runs the two nodes of ekf.launch, the IMU sample validator and robot_localization's EKF, with the parameters that the robot and the simulation use. The test computes their inputs itself: wheel odometry and IMU samples. The test also owns the clock. It publishes the topic /clock and moves the ROS time \(t\) on in steps of 20 ms. So the test decides which messages are published, with which stamps and at which ROS time, without Gazebo and without a robot.

    One step of the clock

    The ROS time of the nodes is the time of the last message on the topic /clock. So the ROS time stands still between two of the test's clock messages, while the computer's own clock, the wall clock, runs on. The test can wait on the wall clock as long as a step needs, and no ROS time passes for the nodes. A step at the start of a scenario, at the ROS time \(t\) = 100.00 s, the test's constant START, runs in five parts:

    1. The inputs: the test publishes a wheel message and an IMU sample, both stamped 100.00 s.
    2. The validator: the test waits on the wall clock until the validator's output, the forwarded IMU sample, has reached the test. The ROS time stays 100.00 s.
    3. The delivery: the test sleeps DELIVERY, 20 ms of wall time, so that the inputs reach the EKF too. The ROS time still stays 100.00 s.
    4. The clock: the test publishes the ROS time 100.02 s, which is STEP, 20 ms, later. The EKF updates 50 times per second of ROS time, so the new clock lets the EKF run its update at 100.02 s, which fuses the two inputs.
    5. The output: the EKF publishes its output at the ROS time 100.02 s, stamped 100.00 s, the time of the inputs that it fused. The test waits on the wall clock until an output arrives that is stamped from the step's 100.00 s up to the clock's 100.02 s.

    The figure shows the messages of this step. Read the figure from top to bottom. The three boxes are the test, the validator and the EKF.     What a pass proves and what a failure means

    Each of the two scenarios is one test that runs its phases in order on one filter, from the filter's start:

    • invalid_imu_samples: a wheel message in every step, 50 times per second of ROS time, as in the simulation, and IMU samples in phases 1 to 6. A pass proves that the estimate of the yaw rate \(\omega_z\) follows the IMU while valid samples arrive, goes to the wheels' yaw rate when none arrive, keeps out the samples that the validator drops, and follows the IMU again after the clock went back. The validator's counts on /diagnostics match the samples that the test sent.
    • robot_rates: the robot's nominal rates: a wheel message in every fifth step, and an IMU sample in every step until the IMU stops in phase 2. A pass proves that the estimate follows the IMU's yaw rate and the wheels' forward speed \(v_x\), also when the speed changes, and that after the IMU stops, each of the 20 measured wheel periods of 100 ms holds one wheel message and the header stamp of exactly one output. The last output then has the wheels' forward speed and yaw rate.

    A failure names the scenario and the assertion. The messages of the phases' checks name their phase, for example "phase 1: the forward speed comes from the wheels". step() fails with "no EKF output for the step at" and the step's ROS time when the test receives no new output stamped from the step's time to the clock's new time within WAIT, 5 s of wall time.

    What the test doesn't show

    • The rates on the robot: robot_rates uses the robot's nominal rates, not measured ones.
    • Whether sensor_timeout fits the robot: the scenario runs with one wheel message per period, without jitter.
    • The two clocks of the robot's inputs: in the test, a step's wheel message and its valid IMU sample have the same stamp.
    • Real sensors: the inputs have no noise, no bias and no delay.
    • The pose and the transform: the test checks the speed, the yaw rate and the output's frames.
    • Where the validator's limits lie: the samples that phases 2 and 4 send to be dropped lie far outside the time limits. The unit tests check the boundary.
    The details
    • The rates on the robot: the scenario robot_rates uses the robot's nominal rates, the IMU at 50 Hz and the wheel odometry at 10 Hz. These rates are not measured on the robot yet. The issue Base: measure the control loop's rate on the robot, then decide (diffbot#218) measures them.
    • Whether sensor_timeout fits the robot: the scenario runs with the robot's sensor_timeout of 0.1 s, the nominal wheel period. The filter runs its update 50 times per second, frequency in ekf.yaml. An update whose queue holds no new measurement predicts without one, and publishes the prediction, when the time since the filter's last measurement computes to at least sensor_timeout (ros_filter.cpp, lines 680–711). In phase 2, only the wheels report, every 0.1 s. The update at a wheel message's stamp runs before that message is in the filter's queue. In the first period, that update still fuses the last IMU sample, and the next update fuses the wheel message. In 8 of the other 19 periods, the time since the last measurement computes to 0.1 s or a little more at that update, and the update publishes a prediction. In the other 11, it computes to a little less, and the next update, 20 ms later, fuses the wheel message. With a larger value, for example 0.12 s, the update at a wheel message's stamp wouldn't predict, and the next update would fuse the message, if the message is in the queue by then. The price: when all inputs stop, the filter predicts first at the first update at which the time since its last measurement computes to at least 0.12 s. With the last measurement at 103.20 s, 103.32 s minus 103.20 s computes to 0.11999999999999034 s, so the first prediction comes at 103.34 s, 0.14 s after the last measurement. On the robot, the value has to cover the wheel period and the delay of the wheel messages. The issue Base: measure the control loop's rate on the robot, then decide (diffbot#218) measures both and sets the value.
    • The two clocks of the robot's inputs: in the test, a wheel message and a valid IMU sample of the same step have the same stamp. On the robot, the wheel odometry is stamped with the time of the control loop, and the IMU's samples with the time of the Teensy, which rosserial keeps in step with the robot's single-board computer (SBC). The test doesn't show how the filter handles a difference between the two.
    • Real sensors: the inputs have no noise, no bias and no delay. How the real gyroscope behaves is a measurement on the robot, as Limits says.
    • The pose and the transform: the test checks the forward speed \(v_x\) and the yaw rate \(\omega_z\) of the filter's output, and the two frame names in the output's message: header.frame_id has to be odom and child_frame_id base_footprint. assert_fused_estimate() checks them in test_ekf_synthetic.py, which the excerpt in the code of one step shows. It doesn't check the position, the heading \(\theta\) or the transform odom → base_footprint on the topic /tf. The test in Gazebo compares the heading with the simulated robot's true turn.
    • Where the validator's limits lie: the samples that phases 2 and 4 send to be dropped are stamped 0.5 s before or after the step's time, far outside max_age, 0.1 s, and max_future, 0.02 s. So the test shows that the running node drops clearly invalid samples, not where the boundary between valid and invalid lies. The validator's unit tests check the boundary: the validator accepts a sample exactly at a limit and rejects one 1 µs beyond it, as Check 4: the two time limits shows.

def scenario_from_arguments(arguments: Sequence[str]) -> Type[SteppedClockTestCase]:
    """Return the scenario that the argument --scenario names.

    arguments may hold others, such as the results file that rostest gives each test node.
    """
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser()
    parser.add_argument('--scenario', choices=sorted(SCENARIOS), required=True)
    known_arguments, _ = parser.parse_known_args(arguments)
    return SCENARIOS[known_arguments.scenario]


if __name__ == '__main__':
    scenario = scenario_from_arguments(rospy.myargv()[1:])
    rospy.init_node('test_ekf_synthetic')
    rostest.rosrun('diffbot_control', 'test_ekf_synthetic', scenario)
    1. rospy.myargv() returns the node's arguments without ROS's remapping arguments, such as the node's name.
    2. scenario_from_arguments() reads --scenario with Python's argparse. parse_known_args() leaves the other arguments alone, such as the path of the result file that rostest adds. The dictionary SCENARIOS maps the name to the scenario's class.
    3. rospy.init_node() starts the test node, and rostest.rosrun() runs the test method of that class and writes its result.

    diffbot_control registers both files with add_rostest() in its CMakeLists.txt, so CI runs them with the workspace's other tests, as Testing and CI describes.

    The inputs

    A wheel message usually reports WHEEL_INPUT, 0.1 m/s and 0.4 rad/s. A valid IMU sample, VALID_SAMPLE, is in the frame imu_link, is stamped with the step's ROS time and reports 0.5 rad/s. A sample that the validator has to drop is in the same frame and reports 2.0 rad/s. Its stamp is too old, repeats the valid sample's stamp, lies in the future or is older than the last valid one. The messages have no noise. Which inputs a step sends depends on the phase, as the tables of invalid_imu_samples and robot_rates list.

    The constants, the wheel message and the IMU samples

    The test publishes on the two topics that the nodes read on the robot. The wheel odometry goes to the EKF on the topic /diffbot/mobile_base_controller/odom. The IMU samples go to the validator on the topic /diffbot/imu/data_raw. The test subscribes to the validator's output, the topic /diffbot/imu/data_validated, to the filter's output, the topic /diffbot/odometry/filtered, and to the topic /diagnostics.

    class ArrivalLog(Generic[Item]):
    """What arrived on a topic, in the order of arrival.

    rospy calls a subscriber's callback in a thread of its own, which appends to the log, while
    the test's thread reads it. A lock guards every access, so that the test's thread never
    reads the log while the callback changes it.
    """

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self._lock = threading.Lock()
        self._items: List[Item] = []

    def append(self, item: Item) -> None:
        """Add an item at the end."""
        with self._lock:
            self._items.append(item)

    def count(self) -> int:
        """Return the number of items so far."""
        with self._lock:
            return len(self._items)

    def since(self, count: int) -> List[Item]:
        """Return a copy of the items after the first count."""
        with self._lock:
            return self._items[count:]

    def contains_all(self, items: Sequence[Item]) -> bool:
        """Return whether each of the items has arrived."""
        with self._lock:
            return all(item in self._items for item in items)
    • append(): adds an item at the end. The subscribers of the filter's output and of the topic /diagnostics have it as their callback, and on_validated() calls it.
    • count(): returns the number of items so far. The test takes this number before a step.
    • since(): returns a copy of the items after the first count items. With the number from before a step, the test gets what arrived since the step started.
    • contains_all(): returns whether each of the given items has arrived.

def outputs_since(self, start: StepStart) -> List[Odometry]:
    """Return the EKF's outputs that arrived after the step's start and belong to the step.

    An output belongs to the step if its stamp lies between the step's time and the ROS time
    that the test has published. An output that arrives late from before the clock went back
    carries a later stamp, and one from an earlier step an earlier stamp.
    """
    return [message for message in self.ekf_outputs.since(start.output_count_before)

def step(self, wheel_input: Optional[WheelInput],
         imu_inputs: Sequence[ImuInput] = ()) -> Odometry:
    """Run one step and return the EKF's last output for it, which has to come.

    One step:
    1. Publish the step's wheel odometry, if wheel_input isn't None, stamped with the step's
       ROS time, and its IMU samples, each stamped with that time plus the sample's offset
       (publish_inputs()).
    2. Wait on the wall clock until the IMU sample validator's output for the step's valid
       samples reaches the test (wait_for_forwarded()). The EKF subscribes to the same
       topic.
    3. Wait DELIVERY more, for the inputs to arrive at the EKF.
    4. Advance the clock by STEP (advance_clock()). The EKF's 50 Hz update then processes the
       inputs.
    5. Wait, with the time limit WAIT, for an EKF output that belongs to the step
       (outputs_since()).
    advance() does 1 to 4.
    """
    start = self.advance(wheel_input, imu_inputs)
    wait_for(lambda: bool(self.outputs_since(start)),
             'no EKF output for the step at {:.2f} s'.format(start.stamp.to_sec()))
    return self.outputs_since(start)[-1]

    The five things of one step, as the docstring of step() numbers them:

    1. Publish the inputs: publish_inputs() publishes the step's wheel odometry, stamped with the step's ROS time, and its IMU samples, each stamped with that time plus the sample's offset. A step whose wheel_input is None publishes no wheel odometry. The scenario robot_rates uses such steps.
    2. Wait for the validator: wait_for_forwarded() waits on the wall clock until the validator's output for the step's valid samples reached the test. The EKF subscribes to the same topic.
    3. Wait DELIVERY: advance() sleeps DELIVERY, 20 ms of wall time, so that the inputs reach the EKF before the clock moves.
    4. Advance the clock: advance_clock() adds STEP to self.now and publishes the new clock. The EKF's update runs 50 times per second of ROS time, so the new clock lets the filter process the step's inputs.
    5. Wait for the EKF: step() waits until the filter published an output that belongs to the step, at most WAIT, 5 s of wall time. step() returns the last such output.

def validator_values(self, array_count_before: int) -> Optional[Dict[str, str]]:
    """Return the validator's latest status values after the first array_count_before arrays.

    None if no array after them has a status of the validator.
    """
    for array in reversed(self.diagnostics.since(array_count_before)):
        for status in array.status:
            if VALIDATOR_STATUS_NAME in status.name:
                return {pair.key: pair.value for pair in status.values}
    return None

def wait_for_validator_diagnostics(self) -> Dict[str, str]:
    """Advance the clock without IMU samples and return the validator's latest values.

    The validator publishes its diagnostics once per second of ROS time.
    """
    array_count_before = self.diagnostics.count()
    start = self.now
    while self.now < start + DIAGNOSTICS_WAIT:
        self.step(WHEEL_INPUT)
    wait_for(lambda: self.validator_values(array_count_before) is not None,
             'phase 7: no status of the IMU sample validator on the topic /diagnostics')
    values = self.validator_values(array_count_before)
    assert values is not None  # wait_for() returned
    return values

def assert_diagnostic_counts(self, values: Dict[str, str]) -> None:
    """Assert that values has the expected count for forwarded and for each drop reason."""
    self.assertEqual(str(self.expected_forward_count), values.get(FORWARDED_KEY),
                     'phase 7: forwarded samples')
    for reason, count in self.expected_drops.items():
        self.assertEqual(str(count), values.get(DROPPED_KEY_PREFIX + reason),
                         'phase 7: dropped, ' + reason)
    self.assertEqual(len(self.expected_drops) + 1, len(values),
                     'phase 7: other drop reasons: {}'.format(values))

    def run_wheel_periods_without_imu(self, count: int) -> List[rospy.Time]:
    """Run count wheel periods without IMU samples and return the times when they start.

    A wheel period is STEPS_PER_WHEEL_MESSAGE steps. Its first step publishes a wheel

    def wait_for_messages_from(self, stamp: rospy.Time) -> List[str]:
    """Wait for a wheel message stamped stamp and an output from stamp on, return the missing.

    The wait lasts at most WAIT s and doesn't fail. Each subscription gets the messages of its
    one publisher in order and runs their callbacks in that order. So when both messages have
    arrived, the callbacks of the earlier wheel messages and outputs have run. The caller moves
    the clock on past stamp first, through a whole wheel period, so that the filter publishes
    an output even when the wheel message reaches it late. Return the names of the messages
    that didn't arrive.
    """
    arrivals: Dict[str, Callable[[], bool]] = {
        'wheel message at {:.2f} s'.format(stamp.to_sec()):
            lambda: self.wheel_message_stamps.contains_all([stamp]),
        'EKF output stamped at {:.2f} s or later'.format(stamp.to_sec()):
            lambda: any(output.header.stamp >= stamp for output in self.ekf_outputs.since(0)),
    }
    wait_until(lambda: all(arrived() for arrived in arrivals.values()))
    return [name for name, arrived in arrivals.items() if not arrived()]

The extra period's outputs are stamped at that end or later, so the phase leaves them out. assert_one_output_per_period() checks that each of the 20 periods contains the header stamp of one output, in their order. A filter that put two output stamps into one period would fail, for example one that predicted again before the next wheel message. In runs of the scenario with changed settings and the wheel messages on time, a sensor_timeout of 0.08 s of ROS time gave 24 outputs for the 20 periods, and predict_to_current_time, which makes the filter predict in every update, gave 100, five in each period. Both runs failed this check. The check doesn't prove the setting itself. In a run in which each of the 20 wheel messages of the measured periods was published 0.5 s of wall time late, a sensor_timeout of 0.08 s gave one output stamp per period, and the run passed. The steps moved the clock on by only 60 ms of ROS time during those 0.5 s, so the test published each message 60 ms of ROS time after its stamp, for example the message stamped 103.00 s at the ROS time 103.06 s. Both sensor_timeout and this delay are ROS times. The test prints how many outputs carry a wheel message's stamp.
    • The forward speed \(v_x\): the last output's, SPEED, 0.1 m/s within 0.002 m/s, with assert_forward_speed(). The estimate comes from phase 1's 0.12 m/s, so the estimate gets back to 0.1 m/s only if the filter fuses the wheel messages of phase 2.
    • The yaw rate \(\omega_z\): the last output's, WHEEL_RATE, 0.4 rad/s, within WHEEL_RATE_TOLERANCE, 0.02 rad/s. The wheel odometry is the filter's only source of the yaw rate in this phase. In the recorded run, the estimate was within the tolerance from the eleventh output on, and 0.4016 rad/s at the end.
