    PID Controllers

    DiffBot uses two PID controllers, one for each motor. Depending on which software version you use the two PID controllers are located in different locations in the source code. Either the two PIDs are in the

    • hardware interface: high-level approach until version 0.0.2, or
    • base controller firmware: low-level approach starting from version 1.0.0

    The current approach (starting from version 1.0.0) operates the PIDs in the low-level base controller firmware.

    In the simulation

    In Gazebo, there is no firmware: gazebo_ros_control controls each wheel's speed with its own PID, with each robot's gains in diffbot_control/config/<model>/pid.yaml. Wheel Dynamics in Simulation explains that PID and why its gains depend on the wheel's inertia and the time step.

    Regardless of the location (high or low-level), each PID is passed the error between velocity measured by the encoders and the target velocity computed by the diff_drive_controller for a specific wheel joint. The diff_drive_controller doesn't have a PID controller integrated, and doesn't take care if the wheels of the robot are actually turning. As mentioned above, ROS Control expects that the commands sent by the controller are actually applied on the real robot hardware and that the joint states are always up to date. This means that the diff_drive_controller just uses the twist_msg on the cmd_vel topic for example from the rqt_robot_steering and converts it to a velocity command for the motors. It doesn't take the actual velocity of the motors into account.

    Note

    See the code of diff_drive_controller where the joint_command_velocity is calculated.

    The feedback loop of one wheel

    A feedback loop measures the value that it controls and corrects the difference to the value that is wanted. Here, the controlled value is a wheel's angular velocity \(\dot{\phi}\), in rad/s. Each wheel has its own loop. Both loops run in the firmware on the Teensy, 20 times per second (UPDATE_RATE_CONTROL in diffbot_base_config.h). In each of these control steps, WheelControl::step() calls two functions of BaseController:

    1. read() measures both wheel speeds with the encoders.
    2. write() computes a new motor command for each wheel with its PID and sends the command to the motor driver. After one second without a wheel command from the Raspberry Pi (COMMAND_TIMEOUT_MS), the firmware stops the motors and skips write() until the next command arrives.

    The figure shows the loop of Remo's left wheel. The right wheel's loop is the same, with its own PID, motor and encoder.

    Each signal has a unit. The signals that the formulas below use also have a symbol:

    Signal Unit
    1 Target wheel speed \(\dot{\phi}_\text{target}\) rad/s
    2 Error \(e\) rad/s
    3 Motor command \(u\) a whole number from −255 to 255
    4 PWM duty cycle %
    5 Rotation of the motor and the wheel rad
    6 Ticks since the previous control step ticks
    7 Measured wheel speed \(\dot{\phi}_\text{measured}\) rad/s

    The blue values in the figure are one control step of Remo's left wheel. In that run, Remo was commanded to drive at 0.2 m/s with its wheels off the ground (bench record). The firmware logged the target, the measured speed, the PID's three terms and the motor command of this step. The PWM duty cycle (4) and the ticks (6) are calculated from the logged values.

    stopWhenCommandExpires() stops the motors once the last wheel command is COMMAND_TIMEOUT_MS old. control_period_ms_ is 50 ms. The control step runs on the first pass of the main loop at which the Teensy's millisecond counter has advanced by at least 50 since the last control step. The encoder measures the actual time between two reads. controller_.read() measures the wheel's speed over the interval since the previous step. controller_.write() then sets the motor command, which normally acts until the next step's write(). So the measured 5.56 rad/s (7) is the result of the previous motor command, and the 96 (3) is the new one. The example takes each interval as the nominal 50 ms. Follow the values around the loop:

    1. Target wheel speed: On the Raspberry Pi, diff_drive_controller converts the commanded robot velocity into a target angular velocity for each wheel. With Remo's wheel radius of 0.033 m, 0.2 m/s is \(\dot{\phi}_\text{target}\) = 0.2 m/s / 0.033 m = 6.06 rad/s. The hardware interface publishes both targets on the topic wheel_cmd_velocities. The firmware stores the left one in wheel_cmd_velocity_left_.

    2. Error: PID::compute() starts with the comparison. Its variable error is the error \(e\), the target minus the measured speed:

      \[ \begin{aligned} e &= \dot{\phi}_\text{target} - \dot{\phi}_\text{measured} \\ &= 6.06\,\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} - 5.56\,\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} = 0.50\,\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \end{aligned} \]

      The error is positive, so the wheel is too slow.

    3. Motor command: PID::compute() turns the error into the motor command \(u\) in two parts. First, it adds three terms. Their sum is the PID's raw output \(u_\text{raw}\):

      \[ \begin{aligned} u_\text{raw} = {} & K_P \cdot e + K_I \cdot \sum e \\ & + K_D \cdot (e - e_\text{previous}) \end{aligned} \]

      \(\sum e\) is the sum of the errors of the control steps so far, and \(e - e_\text{previous}\) is the change of the error since the previous step. The gains are \(K_P = 0.6\), \(K_I = 0.3\) and \(K_D = 0.5\) (K_P, K_I and K_D in diffbot_base_config.h). In the example, the sum is 322.20 rad/s, and the error is the same as in the previous step:

      \[ \begin{aligned} u_\text{raw} = {} & 0.6 \cdot 0.50 + 0.3 \cdot 322.20 \\ & + 0.5 \cdot 0.00 = 96.96 \end{aligned} \]

      Second, the raw output becomes a whole number within the motor driver's range. PID::compute() returns −255 for a raw output below −255, and 255 for a raw output above 255 (PWM_MIN and PWM_MAX). write() stores the returned value in the integer motor_cmd_left_, which drops the fractional part. The result is the motor command \(u\), a whole number from −255 to 255. The sign is the direction, and 255 is the largest amount that the motor driver takes. Here, 96.96 lies within the range, so only the fraction is dropped, and \(u\) = 96. A raw output of 300 would give \(u\) = 255.

    4. PWM duty cycle: AdafruitMotorController::setSpeed() sends the direction and the amount 96 to the motor driver, the Adafruit DC Motor + Stepper FeatherWing. The driver switches the motor's supply voltage on and off many times per second, which is called pulse-width modulation (PWM). The duty cycle is the share of each PWM period during which the voltage is on. For the amount 96, the voltage is on for 96 × 16 of the 4096 steps of each PWM period, a duty cycle of 37.5 % (Adafruit_DCMotor::setSpeed()). The higher the duty cycle, the faster the motor turns.

    5. Rotation: The motor turns the wheel through its gearbox.

    6. Ticks since the previous control step: The encoder in the motor has two Hall sensors. The Teensy counts the edges of their two signals as ticks, 542 for one turn of the wheel (encoder_resolution in base.yaml). The count runs on from one step to the next. Encoder::jointState() reads it and subtracts the count of the previous step. The difference, delta_ticks, is the ticks since the previous step. The debug output doesn't show delta_ticks. With the nominal 50 ms between two steps, the measured speed corresponds to 24 ticks, so the figure shows this calculated count. The count is an estimate: 25 ticks in an interval of 52.1 ms give the same rounded 5.56 rad/s.

    7. Measured wheel speed: Encoder::jointState() converts delta_ticks to an angle and divides the angle by dts, the time since the previous step, which it measures with the Teensy's clock. With 24 ticks in 50 ms: 24 · 2π / 542 = 0.278 rad, and 0.278 rad / 0.05 s = 5.56 rad/s. read() stores this value as joint_state_left_.angular_velocity_ and publishes it to the Raspberry Pi on the topic measured_joint_states. write() then compares it with the target (2).

    The example shows three properties of this PID that matter for tuning:

    • The sum of the errors holds the motor command: It contributes 96.66 of the 96.96. When a wheel has turned at its target speed for several control steps, the error and its change are zero, and so are the first and the third term. Only the sum then keeps the motor command at the value that the motor needs for this speed. When the target is zero and the wheel stands still, the firmware clears the sum.
    • The limit doesn't stop the sum: While the wheel stays slower than its target, each control step adds a positive error to the sum, also while the raw output is above 255 and the motor command stays at 255. This is called integral windup. The stored sum can keep the drive high as the wheel reaches its target. If the wheel overshoots, the error turns negative and starts reducing the sum. After a command timeout, the firmware resets both PIDs and clears their sums.
    • The gains belong to the control rate: The firmware adds the error to the sum once per control step, and it takes the change of the error from one step to the next. It doesn't multiply or divide by the elapsed time between two steps. With another UPDATE_RATE_CONTROL, the same \(K_I\) and \(K_D\) have another effect.

    Driving straight

    diff_drive_controller doesn't use the measured wheel speeds to correct its wheel-speed commands. For this reason a PID controller can help to avoid situations such as the following where the robot moves not straight although it's commanded to do so:

    Note

    The video runs version 0.0.2, operating the PIDs in the high-level hardware interface.

    The PIDs used in the video inherits from the ROS Control control_toolbox::Pid that provides Dynamic Reconfigure out of the box to tune the proportional, integral and derivative gains. The behaviour when using only the P, I and D gains is that the output can overshoot and even change between positive and negative motor percent values because of a P gain that is too high. To avoid this, a feed forward gain F can help to reach the setpoint faster. In the high-level approach this feed forward gain is present as a dynamic reconfigure parameter, defined in the in the pid.yml configuration file in the cfg folder of this package.

    For more details on ROS dynamic reconfigure see the official tutorials.

    With the use of the PID controller the robot is able to drive straight:

    In case of using inexpensive motors like the DG01D-E of DiffBot, you have to take inaccurate driving behaviour into account. The straight driving behaviour can be improved with motors that start spinning at the same voltage levels. To find suitable motors do a voltage sweep test by slightly increasing the voltage and note the voltage level where each motor starts to rotate. Such a test was done on DiffBot's motors.

    Note

    In the videos above, motors numbered 01 and 03 were used coincidencely and I wasn't aware of the remarkable differences in voltage levels. Using the motors 04 and 05 improved the driving behaviour significantly.

    To deal with significant differences in the motors it would also help to tune the two PIDs individually, which is not shown in the video above.

    Note

    Make also sure that the motor driver outputs the same voltage level on both channels when the robot is commanded to move straight. The used Grove i2c motor driver was tested to do this. Another problem of not driving straight can be weight distribution or the orientation of the caster wheel.

    A good test to check the accuracy is to fix two meters of adhesive tape on the floor in a straight line. Then, place the robot on one end oriented in the direction to the other end. Now command it to move straight along the line and stop it when it reaches the end of the tape. Record the lateral displacement from the tape. Measuring a value below 10 cm is considered precise for these motors.

    The video shows the real DiffBot robot as well as running the gazebo_ros_control plugin with diff_drive_controller from ROS Control. The robot is commanded to drive straight on a 2 meter test track. Five runs show different offsets after 2 meter, which can be cause by:

    • Orientation of the start pose
    • Inaccurate motors start to spin at different voltage levels
    • Low encoder resolution (geared output resolution at wheel)
    • Wheel slip or alignment of the left/right and caster wheel
    • Weight distribution
    • Manufacturing tolerances
    • Deformable material of the wheels generating different surfaces of contact with the ground depending on their assembly

    Gain / Trim Model

    In case the robot constantly drives towards one side (e.g., to the left), this issue might be overcome by tuning each PID individually. An alternative solution can be gain and trim parameters, as implemented in the write() method of the high-level hardware interface.

    1
2

    double motor_constant_right_inv = (gain_ + trim_) / motor_constant_;
double motor_constant_left_inv = (gain_ - trim_) / motor_constant_;

joint_velocity_commands_[0] = joint_velocity_commands_[0] * motor_constant_left_inv;
joint_velocity_commands_[1] = joint_velocity_commands_[1] * motor_constant_right_inv;

    The idea is that the trim coefficient \(t\) is a "quick and dirty" way to account for various nuisances of the physical robot, including differences in motors, wheels, and the asymmetry of the robot. It's simple and easily interpretable, too.

    The two PIDs make each wheel turn at its target angular velocity \(\dot{\phi}_{l/r}\), the rate at which the wheel turns about its axle. They don't make sure that the robot drives the commanded path. One reason is that the two wheel radii \(R_{l/r}\) differ slightly. Take a wheel with the radius \(R_{l/r}\) that rolls forward without slipping and turns with a constant \(\dot{\phi}_{l/r}\) for the time \(\Delta t\). It moves over the ground with the speed \(v_{l/r} = R_{l/r} \cdot \dot{\phi}_{l/r}\) and travels the distance \(d_{l/r}\):

    \[ d_{l/r} = v_{l/r} \cdot \Delta t = R_{l/r} \cdot \dot{\phi}_{l/r} \cdot \Delta t \]

    For example, Remo's nominal wheel radius is 0.033 m (geometry.yaml). A wheel with this radius that turns with 2 rad/s for 3 s travels

    \[ d = 0.033\,\text{m} \cdot 2\,\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \cdot 3\,\text{s} = 0.198\,\text{m} \]

    An angle in radians is the ratio of two lengths, so the result is in metres. Remo's two wheels measure 65.5 mm and 66.5 mm in diameter, as the comment in the same file says. With radii of 0.03275 m and 0.03325 m, the same 2 rad/s for 3 s give 0.1965 m and 0.1995 m. One wheel travels 3 mm further, although both wheels turn equally fast. So the robot turns toward the wheel with the smaller radius, which travels the shorter distance.

    These mechanical variations "can't be seen" by the encoders alone. For example assume you want the robot to go straight, you would set the two desired rotational wheel velocities \(\dot{\phi}_{l/r}\)'s to be equal - but still the robot would go left if, e.g., \(R_r > R_l\), because, for the same wheel rotation (ticks), the right wheel has traveled a bigger distance.

    So the question is: what is the control objective?

    When the control objective is to follow a desired reference position (angular or lateral), it is more meaningful to measure that as error driving the PID controller. But nothing stops us from having several PID controllers in cascade sequence. One for the, e.g., lateral position, that computes the desired \((v_0, \omega)\) of the robot, and two more in parallel for the wheel velocities. Still, there would be a trim coefficient to compensate for differences in traveled distances of the two wheels.

    \(d_{l/r}\) are the distances traveled by the left and right wheels, respectively, in a \(\Delta t\):

    \[ d_{l/r} = R_{l/r} \cdot \Delta \phi_{l/r} \]

    where \(\Delta \phi_{l/r}\) is the angle that each wheel turns about its axle in \(\Delta t\), and \(R_{l/r}\) is the radius of each wheel. The equation assumes again that the wheels roll without slipping. The angle is the wheel's, not the motor's. The motor's shaft turns faster than the wheel, by the ratio of the gearbox between them, so the motor's angle times the wheel radius would give too long a distance. If the radii are exactly the same, then \(R_l = R_r = R\) and the approach using only two PID controllers would work because controlling \(\dot{\phi_{l/r}} \approx \frac{\Delta \phi_{l/r,k}}{\Delta t_k}\) is equivalent to controlling \(\Delta \phi_{l/r,k}\), as the time interval we can assume known.

    In practice, \(R_l = R_r = R\) does not hold because of manufacturing tolerances, deformable material of the wheels generating different surfaces of contact with the ground depending on their assembly and robot mass distribution, etc.

    The trim parameter \(t\) we define as something that adjusts the relationship between the commands sent to the motors (imagine voltage \(V\)) and the angular speed of each wheel (\(\dot{\phi}\)):

    \[ \begin{aligned} V_l = k(1-t) \dot \phi_l \\ V_r = k(1+t) \dot \phi_r \end{aligned} \]

    Note that it's the same value of \(t\) for the two motors (\(k\), just assume to be a given constant). The way we tweak it is to find the value such that the robot goes straight, that is, \(d_l = d_r\) (over some distance), when we command it to go straight, that is, we send the same voltages to the two wheels (\(V_l = V_r\)).

    \[ \begin{aligned} V_l &= V_r \\ k(1-t) \dot\phi_l &= k(1+t) \dot\phi_r \end{aligned} \]

    From the two equations (\(V_l\), \(V_r\)), by imposing the assumptions above and using the definition of \(d_{l/r}\), we can derive \(t = \frac{R_r - R_l}{R_r + R_l}\), which basically shows how the trim is there to (empirically) compensate for the difference in the wheel radii.

    source

    The trim / gain approach was customized for Duckiebots. The broader context is system identification: a good resource for further reading could be Russ Tedrake's underactuated robotics course.

    After the robot can drive on the straight line without deviating from it too far the final error can be overcome with additional sensors, such as IMUs, cameras and LiDARs, and then fuse those sensor data to follow a goal location more accurately by constantly adjusting wheel velocities. An even simpler example are IR sensors to follow the line and adjust the wheel velocities according to stay near the line.

    PID Tuning

    To tune the PID controller, the following video provides a helpful overview of the process. It gives some insights which behavior each PID parameter influences for a vehicle trying to follow a lane, thereby reducing the cross track error. However, the same process can be followed to reduce the error \(e(t)\) being the difference between the desired (target) angular wheel velocity \(\dot \phi_{desired,l/r}\) and the one measured \(\dot \phi_{measured,l/r}\) by the encoders:

    \[ e_{l/r} = \dot{\phi}_{desired,l/r} - \dot{\phi}_{measured,l/r} \]

    In case of oscillations (wheel spin direction keeps changing), might happen because of a too high proportional gain \(K_P\) or a too low derivative gain \(K_D\). Changing gains should be done one at a time because all are related by influencing the overall calculated output command \(u(t)\) via the following equation

    \[ u(t) = K_\text{p} e(t) + K_\text{i} \int_0^t e(\tau) \,\mathrm{d}\tau + K_\text{d} \frac{\mathrm{d}e(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

    One option to start the tuning process is by setting these values:

    P = something small until the motors start to spin and no major oscillations are observable I = 0 D = 0

    To overcome oscillations increase the D gain first but usually it will be a value below P (might be one or more magnitudes lower). The derivative term tracks how fast the velocity changes over time. A higher D gain can dampen oscillations and therefore lead to more stable behavior. The D term best estimate of the future trend of the error \(e(t)\). If the derivative gain is too low then the system is called underdamped and will pull too quickly towards the desired velocity, which can easily result in overshooting the target value. On the other hand if the D gain is too high the system is known to be overdamped and it will take a long time to reach the desired velocity. A properly chosen D gain will allow to reach the desired velocity quickly with reduced overshoot and therefore a near zero error rate (known as critically damped).

    Term I accounts for past values of the error and integrates them over time. This term should be adjust last and increased slightly to tune the behavior further.

    In code the values to tune these PID gains are found in the base_controller_config.h:
